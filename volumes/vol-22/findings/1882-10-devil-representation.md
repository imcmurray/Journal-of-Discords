## Sermon Overview

### Main Topic/Purpose
An extensive discourse on priesthood authority, dispensations, temple work, salvation for the dead, and obedience to church courts. The racial theology appears in the context of explaining why wickedness persisted after the flood.

### Key Themes
- The dispensation of the fullness of times
- Pre-mortal existence and Satan's rebellion
- The flood as divine justice
- Priesthood authority across dispensations
- Temple work as saving ordinances
- Obedience to church authority over civil courts
- The perpetuation of Cain's curse through Ham's wife

### Audience
General membership at Provo Stake Conference. Taylor speaks with full presidential authority on doctrinal matters.

### Historical Context
**August 28, 1881** — This is during the height of the priesthood/temple ban era. John Taylor became Church President in October 1880, making this one of his early major discourses as President. The priesthood ban on Black members had been in effect since Brigham Young's presidency and would continue until 1978. Taylor is providing theological justification for a policy already generations old.

The discourse comes during federal pressure over polygamy (the Edmunds Act would pass in 1882), which may explain Taylor's emphasis on church authority over civil courts and his defensive posture toward "Christian ministers."

## Key Quotations

### Quote 1: Black People as "Devil's Representation"

> "And after the flood we are told that the curse that had been pronounced upon Cain was continued through Ham's wife, as he had married a wife of that seed. And why did it pass through the flood? Because it was necessary that the devil should have a representation upon the earth as well as God; and that man should be a free agent to act for himself, and that all men might have the opportunity of receiving or rejecting the truth, and be governed by it or not according to their wishes and abide the result; and that those who would be able to maintain correct principles under all circumstances, might be able to associate with the Gods in the eternal worlds."
> — JoD 22:304

**Context:** Taylor is explaining the theological framework for Noah's flood, pre-mortal spirits, and the continuation of Cain's curse. This comes in a section describing how God destroyed the wicked in the flood to protect righteous pre-mortal spirits from being born into corrupted bodies. He then explains that the curse was deliberately preserved through Ham's wife.

**Significance:** This is arguably the most theologically explicit racist statement in the entire Journal of Discourses. Taylor is not merely justifying a policy or repeating folklore—he is providing systematic theodicy: Black people (descendants of Ham through the "curse of Cain") exist specifically to serve as "the devil's representation upon the earth." This assigns Black people a cosmic role as embodiments of satanic opposition to God's work.

**Category:** PRIMARY: MORAL-PROBLEM, SECONDARY: DISAVOWED, RACIAL

---

### Quote 2: The Flood as Protection from "Corrupt Bodies"

> "Let us suppose that you and I were there as spirits, awaiting the privilege of taking bodies, and that we could see the wickedness and corruption that was going on upon the earth, and that we could see Prophets going about teaching the principles of righteousness and warning the people of judgments that should come, of the flood that should overwhelm them and of the prisons prepared in which the ungodly should be cast. And we say, 'Father, you see the people on the earth that they are wicked and depraved, fallen and corrupt! Yes. Is it right and just that we who have done no wrong should have to enter into such corrupt bodies and partake of the influences with which they are surrounded?' 'No,' says the Father, 'it is not just, and I will cut them off, I will cause the floods to come upon them to destroy them, and I will send those wicked and disobedient spirits into prison,' which he did."
> — JoD 22:303

**Context:** This immediately precedes the "devil's representation" quote. Taylor is explaining the justice of the flood by depicting pre-mortal spirits objecting to being born into "corrupt bodies."

**Significance:** This establishes Taylor's framework that bodies can be inherently "corrupt" based on lineage, setting up the logic that certain bloodlines are spiritually defiled. This racial essentialism—that corruption is inherited bodily—makes the subsequent preservation of Cain's curse through Ham's wife appear as divine design rather than accident.

**Category:** MORAL-PROBLEM, RACIAL

---

### Quote 3: Christians as "Murderers and Murderesses"

> "Say they, 'Look, what a wicked people these "Mormons" are, they have more wives than one. It is true we have mistresses besides our wives; it is true we commit adultery; it is true we are covered with infamy and debauchery; it is true that the stink of our crimes and iniquities rises into the nostrils of Jehovah, as it did in former days, but we will cover all that over.' But they cannot do it; it sticks out on every side; the covering is too narrow. They are murderers and murderesses of their infants, and the stench of their infamy ascends into the nostrils of Jehovah; and you that want them, take them, and you that do will go along with them, and go to perdition with them; and I tell you that in the name of the Lord."
> — JoD 22:303

**Context:** Taylor is attacking Protestant ministers who opposed Mormon polygamy while (he claims) committing adultery and infanticide (likely referring to abortion/birth control). He's warning Latter-day Saints not to send their children to be educated by these ministers.

**Significance:** While not directly related to race, this reveals Taylor's rhetorical pattern: apocalyptic denunciation of outsiders, absolute certainty in divine sanction ("I tell you that in the name of the Lord"), and zero epistemic humility. The same authoritative voice that condemns Christians also explains why Black people are "the devil's representation."

**Category:** SELF-CONTRA (claims moral superiority while preaching racist theology), MORAL-PROBLEM

---

### Quote 4: Curse Deliberately Preserved

> "And after the flood we are told that the curse that had been pronounced upon Cain was continued through Ham's wife, as he had married a wife of that seed. And why did it pass through the flood?"
> — JoD 22:304

**Context:** The crucial question and answer. Taylor explicitly states this was intentional divine design, not accident.

**Significance:** This refutes any apologetic that the curse's continuation was unintentional or merely folkloric. Taylor teaches this as deliberate divine providence: God specifically ensured the curse survived the flood by having Ham marry "a wife of that seed."

**Category:** DISAVOWED, RACIAL, MORAL-PROBLEM

### Self-Contradiction Check

**Internal to this sermon?**
Yes. Taylor condemns Protestant Christians as morally corrupt "murderers and murderesses" whose "crimes and iniquities rises into the nostrils of Jehovah," yet in the same sermon teaches that God deliberately created a race of people to be "the devil's representation." He cannot see that designating an entire race as satanic representatives is itself a profound moral crime.

**Across speaker's other sermons?**
Requires further research. Taylor likely taught similar racial theology elsewhere, but the specific phrasing "devil's representation" may be unique to this sermon.

**Across institution?**
Massive contradiction with modern church teaching:
- **2013 Race and the Priesthood essay** explicitly disavows theories of divine disfavor based on race
- **2020 Church statement**: "any theory that black people are inferior to people of other races stands condemned by the Church"
- **Current church position**: The priesthood ban was a policy, not doctrine, and theories justifying it were incorrect

Taylor's teaching directly contradicts the modern church's position that racism is condemned "unequivocally" and "in any form."

### Irony Potential

**High.** Taylor's own standards condemn him:

1. **"The stench of their infamy ascends into the nostrils of Jehovah"** — What does teaching that Black people are "the devil's representation" smell like to God?

2. **"Murderers and murderesses"** — What do you call someone who teaches that an entire race exists to represent Satan?

3. **"They are covered with infamy and debauchery... but we will cover all that over"** — The modern church has tried desperately to "cover over" this teaching. As Taylor says, "it sticks out on every side; the covering is too narrow."

4. **On Christian ministers**: "They will pull down the pillars of this nation by their mobocracies and infamies"** — What did racist theology like Taylor's do to the pillars of Zion?

5. **"God is looking upon us"** — Indeed. And what does God see when He looks at this sermon?

### Modern Church Position

**2013 Race and the Priesthood Gospel Topics Essay:**

> "Today, the Church disavows the theories advanced in the past that black skin is a sign of divine disfavor or curse, or that it reflects unrighteous actions in a premortal life; that mixed-race marriages are a sin; or that blacks or people of any other race or ethnicity are inferior in any way to anyone else. Church leaders today unequivocally condemn all racism, past and present, in any form."

**Key points:**
1. The essay **disavows theories**, not just practices
2. It specifically rejects the idea that Black skin is "a sign of divine disfavor or curse"
3. It "unequivocally condemns all racism... in any form"

**Problem:** The essay does not name the prophets who taught these theories, does not quote them, and does not explain how these theories—taught by Church Presidents with prophetic authority—could be so completely wrong. The passive voice "theories advanced in the past" obscures that these were not fringe beliefs but official teachings from the highest authorities.

**2020 Statement:**
> "We unequivocally condemn racism, including any and all past racism by individuals both inside and outside the Church."

**Analysis:** The word "unequivocally" does heavy lifting. If teaching that Black people are "the devil's representation upon the earth" isn't racism worthy of explicit repudiation by name, what is?

## Apologetic Anticipation

### Defense #1: "This was just a theory, not official doctrine"

**Why it fails:**
- Spoken by the Church President in an official Conference setting
- Published in the Journal of Discourses, the church's quasi-official record
- Presented as explanation of scripture and divine design, not speculation
- No disclaimer that this was merely Taylor's opinion

**Source rebuttal:**
Taylor introduces the teaching with: "And after the flood **we are told that** the curse... was continued through Ham's wife." This is presented as revealed truth, not personal theory. He follows with "And why did it pass through the flood? **Because it was necessary that the devil should have a representation upon the earth as well as God.**" The word "necessary" indicates divine design, not human speculation.

Moreover, Taylor's entire sermon is about priesthood authority, obedience to prophetic leadership, and God's eternal design. He speaks throughout with absolute authority. To claim this particular passage was "just a theory" requires evidence Taylor flagged it as such—which he did not.

---

### Defense #2: "He was speaking as a man, not as a prophet"

**Why it fails:**
- No textual evidence of this distinction in the sermon
- He was literally the Church President, speaking at Conference
- The sermon is titled "Duties of the Saints" and is about following priesthood authority
- If prophets can be this wrong in official settings, how do we know when they're right?

**Source rebuttal:**
Taylor explicitly grounds his authority throughout: "God expects me to talk plainly. I have not come here to daub you with untempered mortar, but I tell you the truth" (JoD 22:312). He closes: "And while He has called us to high privileges, to thrones and principalities and dominions... in the name of Israel's God we will try and carry it out."

Earlier in the sermon, Taylor emphasizes that Jesus' role was to "regulate the affairs pertaining to the interests of humanity" and that Joseph Smith came "to learn [the Father's] will and do it." Taylor presents himself in this same chain of authority.

If "speaking as a man" renders prophetic teaching void, the entire sermon—and the entire JoD corpus—becomes unreliable. Apologetics cannot pick and choose retroactively.

---

### Defense #3: "This was a product of his time; everyone was racist then"

**Why it fails:**
- Abolitionists existed in 1881; anti-racist views were available
- Many Christians opposed slavery on theological grounds
- Even if culturally common, claiming divine sanction for racism is qualitatively different
- The issue isn't that Taylor was racist, but that he claimed God designed Black people as "the devil's representation"

**Source rebuttal:**
Taylor is not merely reflecting cultural prejudice—he's providing **theological justification for cosmic racial hierarchy**. The claim is not "I personally find Black people inferior" but "God deliberately preserved the curse of Cain through the flood so that the devil would have representation on earth, and Black people are that representation."

This is not passive racism absorbed from culture; it's active theologizing that **makes God the author of racism**. The 13th Amendment passed in 1865; Reconstruction had already failed by 1881. Taylor had access to anti-racist arguments but chose to double down with divine sanction.

Moreover, many of Taylor's "Christian minister" opponents whom he condemns in this very sermon would have rejected his racial theology. If he can hold them to moral account, we can hold him to account.

---

### Defense #4: "The modern church has disavowed this, so it's not a problem"

**Why it fails:**
- Disavowal without explanation creates more problems than it solves
- No acknowledgment that these were teachings from prophets claiming divine authority
- No explanation for how prophets could be so catastrophically wrong
- No apology to Black members or descendants harmed by these teachings
- The passive voice "theories advanced" obscures prophetic responsibility

**Source rebuttal:**
The 2013 essay says the church "disavows the theories advanced in the past" but never names who advanced them or quotes what they said. This is historical gaslighting.

If John Taylor, as Church President, taught that Black people are "the devil's representation upon the earth," and this teaching was wrong, then:
1. How was he sustained as prophet, seer, and revelator?
2. What other prophetic teachings might be equally wrong?
3. Why should members trust current prophets if past prophets were this wrong?

The essay's silence on these questions is damning. Disavowal without accountability is not repentance.

---

### Defense #5: "Context matters—read the whole sermon"

**Why it fails:**
- The full context makes it worse, not better
- The quote is not taken out of context; it's presented exactly as Taylor meant it
- The surrounding material about pre-mortal spirits and divine justice reinforces the racism

**Source rebuttal:**
Reading the full sermon in context shows:

1. **Taylor explicitly states this was divine design**: "And why did it pass through the flood? Because it was necessary that the devil should have a representation upon the earth as well as God."

2. **The theological framework is systematic**: Pre-mortal spirits complained about "corrupt bodies," so God destroyed the wicked in the flood. But God **deliberately preserved** the curse through Ham's wife for a specific purpose: satanic representation.

3. **Taylor claims absolute prophetic authority**: Throughout the sermon, he speaks in the name of God, threatens apostates with perdition, and demands obedience to priesthood authority. The racial teaching is embedded in this authority structure.

4. **The juxtaposition is revealing**: Immediately before the "devil's representation" passage, Taylor condemns Christian ministers as "murderers and murderesses" whose "stench... ascends into the nostrils of Jehovah." Then he explains why God designed Black people to represent the devil. The irony is devastating.

Context does not absolve this teaching. Context condemns it more fully.

---

### Defense #6: "This is anti-Mormon persecution; you're taking this out of proportion"

**Why it fails:**
- This is a direct quote from official LDS sources
- The issue is the teaching itself, not "persecution"
- Calling accountability "persecution" is manipulation
- Black Latter-day Saints have the right to know what prophets taught about them

**Source rebuttal:**
The quote is from the Journal of Discourses, Volume 22, pages 297-312, August 28, 1881. It was transcribed by an official church reporter (Geo. F. Gibbs) and published in the church's semi-official record. This is not "anti-Mormon" material; this is **Mormon** material.

The real question is not "Why are critics bringing this up?" but "Why did the Church President teach this?" and "Why hasn't the church directly addressed and apologized for this specific teaching?"

Accountability is not persecution. Sunlight is not anti-Mormon. Truth is not apostasy.

### Challenges

**Potential pitfalls to avoid:**

1. **Excessive outrage**: The quote is devastating enough; over-commentary weakens it. Let Taylor's words do the work.

2. **Preachiness**: Don't lecture the listener. Present the evidence, show the contradiction, and trust the audience to feel the horror.

3. **Caricature**: Taylor was a real person who believed he was serving God. The song should capture that sincerity—which makes the teaching more horrifying, not less.

4. **Ignoring the victims**: This isn't about scoring points against the church; it's about Black members who lived under this theology. Center their experience.

5. **Apologetic straw-manning**: Present the strongest apologetic defenses before dismantling them. Don't attack weak arguments.

6. **Historical decontextualization**: Yes, Taylor was racist in a racist era—but he claimed **divine sanction** for a **cosmic racial hierarchy**. That's the issue, not mere cultural prejudice.

**Production challenges:**

1. **Tone**: How do you make a song about this without either trivializing the horror or making it unlistenable? Likely answer: Restraint. Understatement. Let the words speak.

2. **Audience**: This will alienate some TBMs no matter how fairly presented. Accept that. The song is for those ready to hear it.

3. **Length**: There's so much material here—flood theology, pre-mortal existence, Ham's wife, divine design, modern disavowal. Resist the urge to include everything. Focus on the core.

**Best approach**: Probably Option 2 (Letter from a Black Member) or Option 5 (Gospel Style). Both center the victims, both create maximum irony, and both avoid the "angry ex-Mormon" tone that lets apologists dismiss the critique.
