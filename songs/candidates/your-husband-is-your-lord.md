## Core Concept

Erastus Snow taught that women cannot enter the celestial kingdom without their husband's permission. A woman must "reverence her husband as her lord; for he is her lord." If she cannot learn to honor him sufficiently, she "ought to have a worse one." Women unworthy of husbands will be received in heaven "as a servant." This song lets Snow's words speak for themselves, revealing the patriarchal framework that made women's eternal salvation dependent on male approval.

## Key Quotes

### The Hook

> "No woman will get into the celestial kingdom, except her husband receives her, if she is worthy to have a husband; and if not, somebody will receive her as a servant."
> — JoD 5:291

### Supporting Quote 1

> "...reverence her husband as her lord; for he is her lord."
> — JoD 5:291

### Supporting Quote 2

> "She may tear herself loose from him and attach another, but she may have a worse one: she ought to have a worse one. If she cannot learn to honour him, the next one she gets, if she is permitted to have another, ought to be a worse one."
> — JoD 5:291

### Supporting Quote 3

> "Do you uphold your husband before God as your lord? 'What!—my husband to be my lord?' I ask, Can you get into the celestial kingdom without him?"
> — JoD 5:291

### Supporting Quote 4

> "Does every woman pray for her children and with her children? Does she teach them to reverence their father and honour him? If she does not teach them thus to honour him in her own words and examples, her children learn disobedience from her."
> — JoD 5:294

- Verse 1: Setting — Utah 1857, the Reformation

- Verse 1: Apostle poses the question

## Notes

The "servant" clause is particularly striking — women without husbands don't just miss out on the highest glory, they become servants to others. This reveals a deep patriarchal structure where women's value is entirely dependent on their relationship to men.

Connection to modern practice: Temple ceremonies included women covenanting to "obey" husbands until 2019 changes. The doctrinal foundation Snow articulates persisted for over 160 years.
