## SOURCE MATERIAL

### Primary Quote 1: The Central Declaration

> "God has given us a revelation in regard to celestial marriage. I did not make it. He has told us certain things pertaining to this matter, and they would like us to tone that principle down and change it and make it applicable to the views of the day. **This we cannot do; nor can we interfere with any of the commands of God to meet the persuasions or behests of men. I cannot do it, and will not do it.**"
> — John Taylor, JoD 25:309 (October 6-7, 1884)

### Primary Quote 2: The Eternal Vow

> "We will stand by the principles of eternal truth; living we will proclaim them, and dying we will be true to them, and after death will live again in their enjoyment in the eternal worlds. That is my feeling; so I don't feel very trembly in the knees, and I do not think you do, generally."
> — John Taylor, JoD 25:309 (October 6-7, 1884)

### Primary Quote 3: The Congregational Ratification

> "If God has introduced something for our glory and exaltation, we are not going to have that kicked over by any improper influence, either inside or outside of the Church of the living God. We will stand by the principles of eternal truth... and all who sanction it say Amen—**[the vast congregation responded with a loud 'Amen.']**"
> — John Taylor, JoD 25:309 (October 6-7, 1884)

### Primary Quote 4: The Nicodemus Mockery

> "I see sometimes a disposition to try to ignore some of the laws which God has introduced, and this is one of them. People want to slip round a corner, or creep out in some way. There is something very creepy about it. There was a man in former times we are told, came to Jesus by night. His name was Nicodemus. He was one of those persons who did not like the daylight... Well, there is not much to such folks."
> — John Taylor, JoD 25:309-310 (October 6-7, 1884)

### Primary Quote 5: The Priesthood Requirement

> "We have also been told that 'it is not mete that men who will not abide my law shall preside over my Priesthood,' and yet some people would like very much to do it. Well, they cannot do it; because if we are here... to do the will of our Father who sent us, and He has told us what to do, we will do it, in the name of Israel's God—and all who sanction it say Amen—**[the vast congregation responded with a loud 'Amen.']**"
> — John Taylor, JoD 25:309 (October 6-7, 1884)

## HISTORICAL CONTEXT

### The Setting

Salt Lake Tabernacle, 54th Semi-Annual General Conference of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, October 6-7, 1884. Church President John Taylor, age 75, addressed thousands of assembled Saints in the most official setting the church could provide.

### The Speaker

John Taylor was the third President of the LDS Church (1880-1887), having succeeded Brigham Young. He was a practicing polygamist with multiple wives. He was present when Joseph Smith was killed at Carthage Jail and was himself wounded, which gave him special authority in Mormon memory. At the time of this sermon, he was the recognized prophet, seer, and revelator of the church.

### The Broader Context

The Edmunds Act of 1882 had criminalized polygamy, and federal marshals were actively prosecuting polygamists. The church was under intense pressure from the U.S. government, with threats of property confiscation and denial of Utah statehood. Taylor was responding to internal and external pressure to compromise on the polygamy doctrine. Some members wanted to "tone down" the practice to avoid persecution.

### Why It Matters

This sermon represents the absolute pinnacle of prophetic certainty. A Church President, at General Conference, with congregational ratification, declared that the church "cannot" change divine commands. He used theological impossibility language ("cannot interfere with commands of God"), not mere preference. The congregation formally voted "Amen" to this commitment.

### The Aftermath

- **Four months later (February 1, 1885):** Taylor himself went "underground" to avoid arrest, becoming the very "Nicodemus" figure he had mocked.
- **Two and a half years later (July 25, 1887):** Taylor died in hiding at the Thomas Roueche home in Kaysville, Utah, still a fugitive but faithful to his vow.
- **Five years, eleven months, eighteen days later (September 24, 1890):** President Wilford Woodruff issued Official Declaration 1 (the Manifesto), doing exactly what Taylor declared they "cannot do."
- **Same congregation** (or their children) that voted "Amen" to never changing voted "Amen" to the Manifesto.

## ADDRESSING APOLOGETIC RESPONSES

### Defense #1: "The Manifesto was divine revelation, so God changed His mind, not the church"

**What They Say:**
Apologetics claim the Manifesto was inspired revelation from God. Therefore, it was God who changed the command, not human surrender to government pressure. The church was simply following new prophetic direction.

**Why It Fails:**
Taylor explicitly said: "we cannot interfere with any of the commands of God to meet the persuasions or behests of men." The Manifesto was a direct response to government pressure (statehood, property confiscation under the Edmunds-Tucker Act)—precisely the "persuasions and behests of men" Taylor said could never change divine commands. If God can change commands based on human pressure, then Taylor's statement that this "cannot" happen was false. This creates an impossible dilemma: Was the Church President at General Conference wrong about the nature of divine commands?

**Source-Based Rebuttal:**
> "This we cannot do; nor can we interfere with any of the commands of God to meet the persuasions or behests of men."
> — JoD 25:309

---

### Defense #2: "Taylor died before the Manifesto; he personally kept his word"

**What They Say:**
Taylor remained faithful until his death in 1887, three years before the Manifesto. He kept his personal vow to "die true." The song should acknowledge his personal integrity.

**Why It Fails:**
Taylor did not speak only for himself. He invoked congregational ratification ("all who sanction it say Amen"), making this an institutional commitment. He spoke as Church President at General Conference (the most official setting possible). He said "men who will not abide my law shall not preside over my Priesthood"—yet his successor stopped abiding. The same congregation (or their children) that voted "Amen" in 1884 voted "Amen" to the opposite in 1890. Taylor spoke for the church, not just himself.

**Source-Based Rebuttal:**
> "We will stand by the principles of eternal truth... and all who sanction it say Amen—[the vast congregation responded with a loud 'Amen.']"
> — JoD 25:309

---

### Defense #3: "He meant morally 'cannot,' not literally impossible"

**What They Say:**
Taylor was making a moral statement about what the church should not do, not a prediction about what would literally never happen. He was expressing moral conviction, not prophesying future events.

**Why It Fails:**
Taylor was responding to active pressure to change ("they would like us to tone that principle down"). He used future-tense promises: "Living we will proclaim them, and dying we will be true to them." The congregational vote created an institutional commitment, not just personal opinion. When a Church President at General Conference invokes God's name, asks for a sustaining vote, and makes absolute declarations about divine commands, it functions as prophecy.

**Source-Based Rebuttal:**
> "Living we will proclaim them, and dying we will be true to them, and after death will live again in their enjoyment in the eternal worlds."
> — JoD 25:309

---

### Defense #4: "Church still believes in plural marriage eternally, just not on earth"

**What They Say:**
The doctrine did not change, only earthly practice was suspended. Celestial plural marriage continues to be believed and practiced (men can be sealed to multiple women after death). The Manifesto was a temporary suspension, not a doctrinal reversal.

**Why It Fails:**
Taylor's context was defense of earthly practice under federal prosecution. "Tone it down" meant stop practicing openly—exactly what the Manifesto did. Modern practice: earthly polygamy results in excommunication (complete reversal of 1884 position). Taylor said "Living we will proclaim"—not just believe privately for the afterlife. The FLDS practices 1884 doctrine on earth and gets excommunicated for it.

**Source-Based Rebuttal:**
> "They would like us to tone that principle down and change it and make it applicable to the views of the day. This we cannot do."
> — JoD 25:309

---

### Defense #5: "Taylor was speaking as a man, not as prophet"

**What They Say:**
Even prophets can make mistakes when not formally prophesying. Taylor's personal opinions do not constitute binding doctrine. He was expressing his feelings, not receiving revelation.

**Why It Fails:**
Taylor was Church President (highest authority) at General Conference (most official setting), speaking about a doctrine already canonized in scripture (D&C 132). He invoked God directly: "He has told us certain things." He asked for a sustaining vote ("all who sanction it say Amen"). He declared "in the name of Israel's God." The sermon was published in the Journal of Discourses (official record). If this setting, authority, and language do not constitute prophetic teaching, what possibly could?

**Source-Based Rebuttal:**
> "We will do it, in the name of Israel's God—and all who sanction it say Amen—[the vast congregation responded with a loud 'Amen.']"
> — JoD 25:309

## THE LOGICAL STRUCTURE

1. **Premise established:** Taylor declares divine commands cannot be changed to meet human pressure ("This we cannot do; nor can we interfere with any of the commands of God to meet the persuasions or behests of men").

2. **Authority invoked:** Taylor speaks as Church President at General Conference, asks for sustaining vote, invokes God's name, and receives congregational "Amen."

3. **Future commitment made:** "Living we will proclaim them, and dying we will be true to them"—a vow applying to the entire institution.

4. **Moral standard set:** Taylor mocks those who "slip round a corner" or come "by night" like Nicodemus, establishing cowardly compromise as shameful.

5. **Ironic fulfillment:** Taylor himself went underground (February 1885), living "by night" for 2.5 years until his death.

6. **Institutional reversal:** The Manifesto (September 1890) did exactly what Taylor said "cannot" be done—toned down polygamy doctrine to meet "persuasions of men."

7. **Congregational contradiction:** Same body that voted "Amen" to never changing voted "Amen" to the Manifesto.

**The Self-Refuting Element:**

John Taylor condemned himself by his own standard in two ways:

1. **Personal irony:** He mocked those who "creep about like Nicodemus by night," then spent his final years in hiding, dying as a fugitive—the very behavior he condemned.

2. **Institutional irony:** He declared the church "cannot" change divine commands to meet human pressure, but six years later the church did exactly that through the Manifesto—proving either (a) these were not divine commands, or (b) the church apostatized, or (c) Taylor was wrong about what prophets can do.

## PRODUCER NOTES

### What This Song Does

This song presents John Taylor's own words from the most authoritative possible setting (Church President at General Conference with congregational ratification), then juxtaposes them with the historical timeline showing Taylor's personal irony (mocked hiding, then hid) and the church's institutional reversal (the Manifesto). The song does not mock or editorialize—it lets the contradiction speak for itself. The tragedy is in Taylor's personal faithfulness (he died true to his vow) contrasted with the institution's abandonment of the commitment he made on its behalf.

### Key Production Decisions

1. **Structure mirrors content:** Confident verses (1-2) represent 1884 certainty; stripped-down bridge presents bare timeline; minor-key verse 3 and final chorus reveal the irony.

2. **Congregational "Amen" as recurring element:** The audience participation ("Amen!") reinforces both the original commitment and the irony of voting "Amen" to opposite positions.

3. **Tragic rather than mocking tone:** Taylor is treated sympathetically—he kept his word until death. The institutional failure is more devastating when shown through his personal faithfulness.

4. **Timeline bridge as turning point:** The spoken dates (1885, 1887, 1890) serve as documentary evidence, letting historical facts create the emotional impact.

5. **Dual voices in outro:** Taylor's "This we cannot do" vs. Woodruff's "We will discontinue" creates the central contradiction without commentary.

### Potential Challenges

- Balancing historical accuracy with musical accessibility
- Avoiding tone of mockery (tragedy is more powerful than satire)
- Making congregational "Amen" feel authentic rather than forced
- The bridge's spoken dates need clear delivery to register
- Final chorus inversion must feel earned, not preachy

### Comparable Works

- Johnny Cash's "Man in Black" (moral conviction, gravitas)
- The National's "Fake Empire" (literary lyrics, building tension)
- Sufjan Stevens' historical narratives (emotional depth, documentary elements)
- Drive-By Truckers' "The Three Great Alabama Icons" (ironic Southern Gothic storytelling)
