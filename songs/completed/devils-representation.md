## SOURCE MATERIAL

### Primary Quote 1: The Devil's Representation

> "And after the flood we are told that the curse that had been pronounced upon Cain was continued through Ham's wife, as he had married a wife of that seed. And why did it pass through the flood? **Because it was necessary that the devil should have a representation upon the earth as well as God**; and that man should be a free agent to act for himself, and that all men might have the opportunity of receiving or rejecting the truth, and be governed by it or not according to their wishes and abide the result; and that those who would be able to maintain correct principles under all circumstances, might be able to associate with the Gods in the eternal worlds."
> — John Taylor, JoD 22:304 (August 28, 1881)

### Primary Quote 2: Pre-Mortal Spirits and Corrupt Bodies

> "And it was also said that the spirits which were in heaven, who had remained faithful, and who had kept their first estate, some of them became very much troubled when they saw the bodies of men corrupted and made contemptible, and they cried out to the Lord, asking him if it was necessary that they who had done no wrong should have to enter into such corrupt bodies."
> — John Taylor, JoD 22:303 (August 28, 1881)

### Primary Quote 3: Taylor's Authority Claim

> "God expects me to talk plainly. I have not come here to daub you with untempered mortar, but I tell you the truth."
> — John Taylor, JoD 22:312 (August 28, 1881)

### Primary Quote 4: The Flood as Divine Judgment

> "Then the Lord said—I will cause the floods to come upon them to destroy them, and I will send those wicked and disobedient spirits into prison, for they shall not live again upon the earth until they have paid the uttermost farthing."
> — John Taylor, JoD 22:303 (August 28, 1881)

## HISTORICAL CONTEXT

### The Setting

John Taylor delivered this sermon at the Provo Stake Conference on August 28, 1881. It was an official church gathering, with Taylor speaking as the sitting Church President. The sermon was transcribed by George F. Gibbs, the official church reporter, and published in Volume 22 of the Journal of Discourses—the semi-official record of church teachings from 1854-1886.

### The Speaker

John Taylor was the third President of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, serving from 1880 until his death in 1887. He had been present at the martyrdom of Joseph Smith in 1844, where he was wounded. By 1881, he was the most authoritative living voice in the church, speaking with the full weight of prophetic office.

### The Broader Context

The priesthood and temple restrictions against Black members had been in effect informally since Brigham Young's administration. By 1881, these restrictions were firmly established. Taylor's sermon provides theological justification for why God would allow such restrictions—not as a human policy error, but as divine design. The 13th Amendment had been ratified in 1865, and Reconstruction had ended in 1877, meaning Taylor was speaking in a post-slavery America where abolitionist arguments had already triumphed legally.

### Why It Matters

This may be the most extreme racist theological statement in the entire Journal of Discourses. Taylor doesn't merely defend a policy—he claims that God deliberately preserved the "curse of Cain" through Noah's flood specifically so that "the devil should have a representation upon the earth as well as God." This designates an entire race as literally representing Satan, with divine intentionality behind their existence as such. Black Latter-day Saints who lived under the priesthood ban were, according to this theology, born to represent the devil.

### The Aftermath

The priesthood and temple restrictions remained in place for 97 years after this sermon, until Official Declaration 2 in 1978. In 2013, the church published a Gospel Topics essay titled "Race and the Priesthood" which states: "Today, the Church disavows the theories advanced in the past that black skin is a sign of divine disfavor or curse, or that it reflects unrighteous actions in a premortal life." However, the essay uses passive voice throughout, never naming John Taylor or quoting his specific teaching. No formal apology has been issued to Black members who lived under this theology.

## ADDRESSING APOLOGETIC RESPONSES

### Defense #1: "This was just a theory, not official doctrine"

**What They Say:**
Taylor was expressing personal opinion, not speaking as prophet. The Journal of Discourses contains speculation, not binding doctrine. Members aren't required to believe everything in the JoD.

**Why It Fails:**
- Spoken by the Church President at an official Stake Conference
- Published in the church's semi-official record of prophetic teachings
- Presented as explanation of scripture and divine design, not flagged as speculation
- Taylor says "we are told that" (revealed truth) and "it was necessary" (divine design)
- The entire sermon is about priesthood authority and following prophetic leadership
- Taylor explicitly claims divine authorization: "God expects me to talk plainly"

**Source-Based Rebuttal:**
> "God expects me to talk plainly. I have not come here to daub you with untempered mortar, but I tell you the truth."
> — JoD 22:312

---

### Defense #2: "He was speaking as a man, not as a prophet"

**What They Say:**
Even prophets can err when speaking as men. Not everything a prophet says is doctrine. This doesn't represent official church teaching.

**Why It Fails:**
- No textual evidence Taylor distinguished between "man" and "prophet" mode in this sermon
- He was literally the Church President speaking at Conference
- He explicitly claims divine authority throughout the sermon
- If prophets can be this catastrophically wrong in official settings while claiming to speak truth from God, the doctrine of prophetic guidance becomes meaningless
- Members at the time had no mechanism to distinguish "prophet mode" from "man mode"

**Source-Based Rebuttal:**
> "God expects me to talk plainly. I have not come here to daub you with untempered mortar, but I tell you the truth."
> — JoD 22:312

The question becomes: if a Church President at Conference, claiming to speak plainly for God, teaching systematically from scripture, can teach that an entire race represents the devil—how would anyone know when he's speaking as a prophet?

---

### Defense #3: "Product of his time—everyone was racist then"

**What They Say:**
We can't judge 19th-century figures by modern standards. Taylor reflected the cultural norms of his era. Most people in 1881 held racist views.

**Why It Fails:**
- Abolitionists existed and had triumphed legally by 1881 (13th Amendment, 1865)
- Anti-racist views were available and articulated by many contemporaries
- Taylor isn't merely reflecting cultural prejudice—he's claiming **God designed racism**
- Making God the author of a cosmic racial hierarchy is qualitatively different from personal bias
- Many of the "Christian ministers" Taylor condemns elsewhere in this sermon likely rejected his racial theology
- The defense acknowledges the teaching was racist while trying to excuse it

**Source-Based Rebuttal:**
Taylor's argument isn't "Black people are inferior" (cultural racism). It's "God deliberately designed Black people to represent the devil on earth." This is a theological claim about divine design, not merely a reflection of cultural attitudes. The distinction matters: cultural racism can be excused as ignorance; claiming God is a racist implicates the entire prophetic truth-claim.

---

### Defense #4: "The church has disavowed this, so it's resolved"

**What They Say:**
The 2013 Gospel Topics essay explicitly rejects these theories. The church has moved on. Continuing to bring this up is dwelling on the past.

**Why It Fails:**
- The essay uses passive voice: "theories advanced in the past"—never names Taylor
- No direct quote of what prophets actually taught, preventing members from knowing the full scope
- No explanation for how a prophet claiming divine authority could teach that an entire race represents Satan
- No apology to Black members who lived under this theology for generations
- Disavowal without accountability is not repentance
- "Moving on" requires first naming what happened

**Source-Based Rebuttal:**
Compare the teaching: "the devil should have a representation upon the earth as well as God" (JoD 22:304) with the disavowal: "the Church disavows the theories advanced in the past" (2013 essay). The passive voice hides the active teaching. Black members who ask "what exactly did the prophets teach about us?" deserve a direct answer.

---

### Defense #5: "You're taking it out of context"

**What They Say:**
Reading the full sermon shows this quote is being misrepresented or cherry-picked. Context would clarify Taylor's meaning.

**Why It Fails:**
- The full context makes it **worse**, not better
- Taylor explicitly states this was divine design: "And why did it pass through the flood? Because it was **necessary**..."
- The theological framework is systematic: pre-mortal spirits, flood theology, Ham's wife, divine preservation of the curse
- Immediately before this quote, Taylor describes pre-mortal spirits objecting to being born into "corrupt bodies"—establishing his framework that bloodlines carry spiritual pollution
- He speaks with absolute prophetic authority throughout
- No contextual reading can make "the devil should have a representation upon the earth" less damning

**Source-Based Rebuttal:**
> "And why did it pass through the flood? Because it was necessary that the devil should have a representation upon the earth as well as God."
> — JoD 22:304

Taylor asked the question himself and gave a systematic theological answer. There is no exculpatory context.

### Defense #6: "This is anti-Mormon persecution"

**What They Say:**
Critics are using selective quotes to attack the church. This is bigotry against Latter-day Saints. You're just trying to make the church look bad.

**Why It Fails:**
- This is a direct quote from official LDS sources (JoD 22:304)
- Transcribed by the official church reporter (Geo. F. Gibbs)
- Published in the church's semi-official record of prophetic teachings
- Black Latter-day Saints have the right to know what prophets taught about them
- Calling accountability "persecution" is a form of manipulation
- Truth-telling is not bigotry

**Source-Based Rebuttal:**
If quoting a Church President from official church publications constitutes "persecution," then the church's own historical record persecutes the church. The problem isn't the quotation—it's the teaching itself.

## THE LOGICAL STRUCTURE

1. **Premise 1:** John Taylor was the Church President and claimed to speak divine truth ("God expects me to talk plainly... I tell you the truth")
2. **Premise 2:** Taylor taught that God deliberately preserved the "curse of Cain" through Noah's flood via Ham's wife
3. **Premise 3:** Taylor explained the reason: "Because it was necessary that the devil should have a representation upon the earth as well as God"
4. **Premise 4:** This designates an entire race as representing Satan, by divine design
5. **Conclusion:** Either Taylor was speaking for God (making God a racist), or Taylor was catastrophically wrong while claiming prophetic authority (undermining the reliability of prophetic guidance)

**The Self-Refuting Element:**

Taylor claims to speak plainly for God, telling "the truth" without "untempered mortar." Yet he teaches something the modern church calls false and "disavows." If Taylor was speaking for God, God taught racism. If Taylor wasn't speaking for God while claiming to, prophetic authority is unreliable. Either way, the teaching condemns the authority structure that produced it.

The deeper irony: in the same sermon, Taylor condemns Protestant ministers as "murderers." He cannot see that designating an entire race as representing the devil is itself a profound moral crime—one he accuses others of in different forms.

## PRODUCER NOTES

### What This Song Does

This song presents John Taylor's 1881 teaching in his own words, with minimal editorializing. It establishes the prophetic authority structure (Church President, official Conference, claimed divine guidance), presents the core teaching ("the devil should have a representation upon the earth"), and juxtaposes it with the modern church's passive-voice disavowal. The song lets the horror speak for itself through accurate quotation and careful framing.

### Key Production Decisions

1. **Somber tone, not satirical:** The subject matter demands gravity. Mockery would undercut the moral weight. The song should feel like witness testimony, not comedy.

2. **Gospel music irony:** Using Black gospel musical tradition (Hammond organ, choir) to indict anti-Black theology creates devastating but respectful irony. This is reclamation, not mockery.

3. **Bridge addresses modern church:** Without the bridge, the song is merely historical documentation. The bridge holds the current church accountable for its passive-voice disavowal that refuses to name Taylor or quote his teaching.

4. **Restraint over spectacle:** Taylor's words are so extreme they need no embellishment. Production should leave space for the lyrics to land, not compete with them.

5. **97-year framing:** The final chorus connects Taylor's 1881 teaching to the 1978 revelation, spanning nearly a century of harm based on this theology.

### Potential Challenges

- **Tone calibration:** The song must be heavy without being melodramatic, accusatory without being shrill
- **Choir use:** The gospel choir is ironic, but must be executed with respect, not as caricature
- **Avoiding exploitation:** This is about holding prophets accountable, not using Black suffering for entertainment
- **Suno limitations:** AI-generated music may struggle with the specific gravitas required

### Comparable Works

- Billie Holiday, "Strange Fruit" — Horror through restraint
- Nina Simone, "Mississippi Goddam" — Righteous anger with musical control
- Johnny Cash, "Man in Black" — Moral witness through stark simplicity
- Mahalia Jackson's spirituals — Gravitas and reverence for serious subjects
