## SOURCE MATERIAL

### Primary Quote 1: The Mark of Cain

> "You see some classes of the human family that are black, uncouth, un-comely, disagreeable and low in their habits, wild, and seemingly deprived of nearly all the blessings of the intelligence that is generally bestowed upon mankind. The first man that committed the odious crime of killing one of his brethren will be cursed the longest of any one of the children of Adam. Cain slew his brother. Cain might have been killed, and that would have put a termination to that line of human beings. This was not to be, and the Lord put a mark upon him, which is the flat nose and black skin."
> — Brigham Young, JoD 7:290-291 (October 9, 1859)

### Primary Quote 2: The "Last Ones" Prophecy

> "Trace mankind down to after the flood, and then another curse is pronounced upon the same race—that they should be the 'servant of servants;' and they will be, until that curse is removed; and the Abolitionists cannot help it, nor in the least alter that decree. How long is that race to endure the dreadful curse that is upon them? That curse will remain upon them, and they never can hold the Priesthood or share in it until all the other descendants of Adam have received the promises and enjoyed the blessings of the Priesthood and the keys thereof. Until the last ones of the residue of Adam's children are brought up to that favourable position, the children of Cain cannot receive the first ordinances of the Priesthood. They were the first that were cursed, and they will be the last from whom the curse will be removed."
> — Brigham Young, JoD 7:290-291 (October 9, 1859)

### Primary Quote 3: The Internal Contradiction ("One Blood")

> "God has created of one blood all the nations and kingdoms of men that dwell upon all the face of the earth: black, white, copper-coloured, or whatever their colour"
> — Brigham Young, JoD 7:290 (October 9, 1859)

### Primary Quote 4: The 2013 Disavowal

> "Today, the Church disavows the theories advanced in the past that black skin is a sign of divine disfavor or curse, or that it reflects unrighteous actions in a premortal life; that mixed-race marriages are a sin; or that blacks or people of any other race or ethnicity are inferior in any way to anyone else. Church leaders today unequivocally condemn all racism, past and present, in any form."
> — "Race and the Priesthood" Gospel Topics Essay, 2013

## HISTORICAL CONTEXT

### The Setting

The sermon was delivered on October 9, 1859, in the Tabernacle at Great Salt Lake City. It was titled "Intelligence, Etc." and was transcribed by G.D. Watt for publication in the Journal of Discourses, Volume 7, pages 282-291. This was a regular Sunday service address to the general membership of the LDS Church.

### The Speaker

Brigham Young was serving as the second President of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, a position he held from 1847 until his death in 1877. He was also the Governor of the Utah Territory. Young spoke with full prophetic authority from the Tabernacle pulpit. His teachings were published in the Journal of Discourses, which was sold as a subscription series and treated as semi-official church literature.

### The Broader Context

The sermon was delivered in 1859, two years before the American Civil War began. The abolitionist movement was strong and growing. Young explicitly dismisses abolitionists in this sermon, claiming divine authority superseded their efforts: "the Abolitionists cannot help it, nor in the least alter that decree." This shows Young was aware of anti-slavery arguments but rejected them on religious grounds.

The LDS priesthood restriction for Black members had begun either in 1849 or 1852 (scholars debate the exact date). This sermon provided theological justification for the policy by identifying specific physical features as divine curses and making prophetic claims about when Black people could receive priesthood.

### Why It Matters

This teaching represents one of the most explicit statements in LDS history identifying physical features as divine curses. For 119 years (until 1978), this theology was used to deny priesthood ordination and temple ordinances to Black members. Families could not be sealed together. The harm was generational.

The 2013 "Race and the Priesthood" essay explicitly disavows "theories...that black skin is a sign of divine disfavor or curse." This institutional admission validates the criticism: prophets taught racist theology as divine truth for over a century.

### The Aftermath

- **1978:** Official Declaration 2 extended priesthood to all worthy male members regardless of race
- **No apology:** The 1978 revelation included no apology or explanation for the 119-year restriction
- **Failed prophecy:** Young's "last ones" timeline was proven false - millions of non-Black people have never received priesthood ordinances, yet Black members can now receive them
- **2013 essay:** The LDS church disavowed the racial theories but called them "theories" rather than acknowledging they were taught as doctrine
- **Ongoing harm:** Many members remain unaware of this history; racist folk doctrine persists in some communities

## ADDRESSING APOLOGETIC RESPONSES

### Defense #1: "He was speaking as a man, not a prophet"

**What They Say:**
Prophets can express personal opinions that don't represent official doctrine. When Young made these statements, he was sharing his own views, not receiving revelation.

**Why It Fails:**
- Delivered from the Tabernacle pulpit as President of the Church
- Published in official church publication (Journal of Discourses)
- Presented as doctrinal teaching with prophetic authority
- Made specific prophetic claims about priesthood timing ("until the last ones")
- Used for 119 years to justify institutional policy
- Never corrected during his lifetime or for over a century afterward

**Source-Based Rebuttal:**
> "The Abolitionists cannot help it, nor in the least alter that decree."
> — JoD 7:291

Young explicitly claims this is God's decree, not personal opinion. The word "decree" indicates divine mandate.

---

### Defense #2: "It was a product of his time - everyone was racist then"

**What They Say:**
We can't judge 19th-century figures by modern standards. Racism was common in 1859; Young was simply reflecting the attitudes of his era.

**Why It Fails:**
- The abolitionist movement was strong in 1859; many opposed slavery and racism
- Other denominations did not claim physical features were divine curses
- Most 19th-century racism was social/cultural, not claimed as divine revelation
- Young explicitly dismisses abolitionists and claims divine authority for his position
- Prophets are supposed to receive revelation that transcends cultural limitations

**Source-Based Rebuttal:**
> "The Abolitionists cannot help it, nor in the least alter that decree."
> — JoD 7:291

If prophets merely reflect cultural prejudices rather than receiving revelation, then prophetic authority is meaningless. Young knew there were alternatives; he rejected them on religious grounds.

---

### Defense #3: "The priesthood ban is over now, so this doesn't matter"

**What They Say:**
The 1978 revelation corrected this. The past is the past. What matters is that all worthy members can now receive priesthood ordinances.

**Why It Fails:**
- The ban lasted 119 years, affecting millions of people
- No apology or explanation was given in 1978
- Young's "last ones" prophecy was proven false
- Generational trauma persists in Black LDS families
- The theology used to justify racism was taught as revelation for over a century
- If this represents 119 years of prophetic error, it raises fundamental questions about prophetic reliability

**Source-Based Rebuttal:**
> "They were the first that were cursed, and they will be the last from whom the curse will be removed."
> — JoD 7:291

This was a testable prophetic claim. It failed. The harm cannot be undone by simply ending the policy.

---

### Defense #4: "The 2013 essay disavowed these teachings - the church has corrected itself"

**What They Say:**
The church acknowledged the problem and officially disavowed the racist theories. This shows institutional willingness to correct past errors.

**Why It Fails:**
- The essay calls them "theories," not acknowledging they were taught as doctrine
- No apology to Black members or their ancestors
- The essay is unsigned and carries less authority than the original teachings
- Does not explain HOW prophets taught false doctrine as revelation for 119 years
- Many members remain unaware; racist folk doctrine persists

**Source-Based Rebuttal:**
> "Today, the Church disavows the theories advanced in the past that black skin is a sign of divine disfavor or curse..."
> — "Race and the Priesthood" essay, 2013

The essay's existence proves the criticism's validity. But true correction requires institutional repentance, not just a quiet, unsigned essay.

## THE LOGICAL STRUCTURE

1. **Premise 1:** Young taught that Black physical features ("flat nose and black skin") are divine curses placed by God
2. **Premise 2:** Young prophesied that Black people could not receive priesthood until "all the other descendants of Adam" received it first - "the last ones"
3. **Premise 3:** In 1978, Black members received priesthood access while millions of non-Black people still had not
4. **Premise 4:** In 2013, the LDS church officially disavowed the teaching that "black skin is a sign of divine disfavor or curse"
5. **Conclusion:** Either Young was wrong about God's nature and actions (prophetic error), or God changed His mind about race (theological instability)

**The Self-Refuting Element:**

Earlier in the same sermon, Young stated: "God has created of one blood all the nations and kingdoms of men that dwell upon all the face of the earth." He then immediately taught that some races are divinely cursed with inferior features and "deprived of nearly all the blessings of the intelligence." The sermon contradicts itself.

Additionally, the "last ones" prophecy creates an impossible situation:
- If Young spoke as a prophet, his prophecy failed
- If he spoke as a man, prophets can lead the church astray for over a century
- Either way, prophetic authority is compromised

## PRODUCER NOTES

### What This Song Does

This song serves as testimony to theological racism, not satire. It lets Brigham Young's own words speak plainly, documenting:
1. The explicit teaching that physical features are divine curses
2. The failed prophecy about priesthood timing
3. The internal self-contradiction ("one blood" vs. cursed races)
4. The 119 years of institutional harm
5. The eventual disavowal

The somber, spiritual tone honors the gravity of harm done to Black Latter-day Saints while creating space for reckoning with institutional racism.

### Key Production Decisions

1. **Musical style:** Blues spiritual (not satirical) - References Blind Willie Johnson, Nina Simone's "Strange Fruit," Mahalia Jackson. This is testimony, not entertainment.

2. **Vocal approach:** Speak-singing, weathered voice, sparse delivery. Let the words carry weight. Not trying to "perform" - just testifying.

3. **Instrumentation:** Acoustic guitar (fingerpicked), minimal percussion (brushes), upright bass. Possible organ for gospel undertones. Dry, spacious mix.

4. **Structure:** Begins with the "one blood" statement (establishing the contradiction), builds through the teaching and prophecy, includes the bridge documenting 119 years of harm, ends with the disavowal - unresolved, bearing witness.

5. **Lyrical choices:** Uses exact quotes where possible ("flat nose and black skin," "uncouth, un-comely, disagreeable," "the last ones"). Paraphrases are clearly traceable. No embellishment beyond what the source material supports.

### Potential Challenges

- **Tonal balance:** Must be somber witness without becoming exploitative of pain
- **Length:** Standard version at ~3 minutes may feel rushed; extended version may lose focus
- **Vocal delivery:** Need weathered, authentic voice - not polished or performative
- **Closing:** Song ends unresolved intentionally; may feel incomplete to some listeners

### Comparable Works

- Blind Willie Johnson - "Dark Was the Night, Cold Was the Ground" (wordless testimony)
- Nina Simone - "Strange Fruit" (somber witness to racial violence)
- Mahalia Jackson - "Trouble of the World" (gospel weight and authority)
